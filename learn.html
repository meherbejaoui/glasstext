How these numbers work

Every measurement glasstext reports is a ratio of things it counted, run through an equation someone published between 1948 and 2010. This page explains what each one is doing, where it came from, and — the part most tools skip — the specific circumstances under which it will lie to you.

Readability: two ingredients, six recipes

Between the 1920s and the 1970s, researchers tried to predict how hard a passage would be to understand. The method was consistent: take passages, test readers on comprehension, then fit a regression from countable features of the text to the test scores.

What survived is a family of formulas that share a striking property. Every one of them uses exactly two ingredients:

  1. A sentence-length term — words per sentence.
  2. A word-difficulty term — syllables per word, letters per word, or the proportion of words with three or more syllables.

That is the whole input. Flesch Reading Ease weights them one way, Flesch–Kincaid another, Gunning Fog a third. The Coleman–Liau index uses letters instead of syllables specifically so a 1975 computer could score text without a pronunciation dictionary. But the raw material is identical, which is why the six formulas usually agree — and why, when they disagree, it tells you something. A wide spread nearly always means sentence length and word length are pointing in opposite directions: short sentences full of long words, or long sentences of plain ones.

Because they share ingredients, agreement between formulas is not corroboration. Six thermometers built from the same broken sensor still agree.

Grade levels are not grades

Five of the six report a "US grade level". This is a calibration artefact, not a measurement of schooling. Flesch–Kincaid in particular was fitted on US Navy technical manuals and enlisted personnel in 1975. When it prints 11.4 for your text, the honest reading is "about as syntactically heavy as material that tested at that level, for those readers, in that decade" — not "a sixteen-year-old will understand this".

Grades above roughly 16 are extrapolation. The formulas are linear and unbounded, so a single 200-word sentence will happily produce a grade level of 80. That number means "your sentence is very long", and nothing else.

The syllable problem, and how accurate ours is

Four of the six formulas need a syllable count. English spelling does not determine one. Wind is one syllable; winded is two. Fire, hour and real are argued over by native speakers. Every readability tool you have used contains a heuristic here, and almost none of them tell you how good it is.

Ours is a rule-based counter: vowel-group counting, plus rules for silent terminal e, syllabic l and r, the -tion family, vowel hiatus, and syllabic consonants in rhythm and prism. Each rule was added in response to a measured error cluster, not a hunch.

We scored it against the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary, where the ground truth is the number of stress-bearing vowel phonemes in the recorded pronunciation:

MeasureResult
Words scored117,486
Exact match91.98%
Within ±1 syllable99.77%
Mean absolute error0.083
Mean signed error−0.023

Two caveats we would rather state than have you discover. First, CMUdict is a speech-recognition dictionary heavy with proper nouns and surnames, which are harder than ordinary vocabulary — so this figure is pessimistic for normal prose. Second, the residual error is not random: the counter is very slightly biased low, so texts full of long words get readability scores fractionally kinder than they deserve.

You can reproduce all of it: npm run validate:syllables.

Does anyone else get the same answers?

The residual gaps are syllable-counting and character-counting differences, not disagreements about the formulas. Flesch Reading Ease shows the largest gap because it multiplies syllables-per-word by 84.6, amplifying any difference. This is worth internalising generally: when two readability tools disagree, it is almost never the equation. It is the tokenizer.

Why the type-token ratio measures length

The obvious way to measure vocabulary richness is the type-token ratio: distinct words divided by total words. It is also close to useless for comparing texts, and the reason is structural rather than fixable.

Function words — the, of, and, to — recur no matter how inventive the writing is. As a text lengthens, the denominator grows steadily while the numerator grows ever more slowly. So TTR falls with length for every text ever written. Compare a 200-word note with a 5,000-word essay by the same author and you will "discover" that the note has a richer vocabulary.

The Distribution tab draws this directly as Heaps' law. The vocabulary growth curve bends; TTR is just that curve's height divided by its horizontal position; a bending curve guarantees a falling ratio.

The two standard fixes

MATTR (Covington & McFall, 2010) holds the window fixed: compute TTR over every window of 50 consecutive words and average. Because all windows are the same length, length cancels out, and a long text becomes directly comparable to a short one.

MTLD (McCarthy & Jarvis, 2010) inverts the question. Instead of asking "what is the ratio at this length?", it asks "how many words does it take for the ratio to fall to 0.720?" It reads forward counting that run, resets, repeats, then does the whole thing backwards and averages. Richer text sustains longer runs. The 0.720 threshold is empirical — it is where TTR curves flatten out.

Neither is magic. MTLD is unstable below about 100 words, and both measure surface forms: run and running count as two different words, because none of this involves a lemmatizer. glasstext says so on the panel rather than in a footnote.

Zipf, Heaps, and the danger of a good fit

Rank the words of almost any text by frequency, plot rank against frequency on log-log axes, and you get a straight line with a slope near −1. This is Zipf's law, and it is genuinely remarkable that it holds across languages, authors, and centuries.

It is also much weaker evidence than it appears. Piantadosi's 2014 review shows that a very wide class of processes produce Zipf-like distributions, including a monkey typing randomly on a keyboard with a space bar. A good Zipf fit tells you a text has the statistical shape of running prose. It tells you nothing about whether the text means anything.

We show it anyway, for two honest reasons. It makes the vocabulary numbers on the previous tab intuitive rather than abstract; and a badly broken fit is genuinely diagnostic — word lists, tables, and heavily templated text visibly leave the line.

One disclosure about our own method: we fit by least squares in log-log space. This is the standard quick approach and it is statistically sloppy, because it weights the long noisy tail of once-occurring words as heavily as the well-estimated head. Maximum-likelihood estimation would be more defensible. We use the log-log fit because it is the line you can actually see on the chart, and we would rather tell you that than let you assume otherwise.

The Scunthorpe problem: why wordlist filters never worked

Screening text against a list of banned words is among the most reimplemented ideas in software, and among the most reliably broken. It looks like a solved problem — search for the word, flag the match — and the failure only becomes visible once real people are typing into it.

In 1996, AOL's filter refused to let residents of Scunthorpe, England, create accounts. Their town's name contains a substring the filter objected to. The bug is named after them, and it has been reshipped continuously ever since — by Yahoo, by academic paper submission systems, by comment platforms.

The Filter lab lets you run the mechanism yourself. The demonstration list contains only ordinary English words — hell, ass, anal, sex, cum — chosen precisely because they are the documented causes of famous over-blocking. glasstext ships no profanity list; the mechanism and its failure modes are identical whatever words you put in.

What you will find is a trap with three corners, and you cannot occupy more than one:

ApproachCatches evasion?False positives
Substring matchingSomeCatastrophic — class, analysis, document, Sussex
Whole-word matchingNo — h3ll, h e l l, Cyrillic һеӏӏNone
Normalise, then matchMostSome return, by construction

The normalisation pipeline is worth understanding because it generalises far beyond profanity — it is the same machinery you need for search, deduplication, and username uniqueness. Order matters: strip invisible characters first or they block every later rule; apply Unicode NFKC to fold compatibility forms; fold homoglyphs deliberately, because Cyrillic а (U+0430) is a genuinely different letter from Latin a and Unicode will never fold it for you; then do the lossy steps last.

Machine learning does not dissolve the problem

The natural response is "use a classifier instead". Two findings should temper that:

  • Hosseini et al. (2017) defeated Google's production Perspective API with typos. Character-level noise that a human reads through without noticing collapsed the model's confidence.
  • Sap et al. (2019) found that hate-speech classifiers flag African-American English at substantially higher rates. A filter deployed naively does not moderate neutrally; it penalises hardest the people already moderated most.

This is why glasstext's screening panel returns matches and never a verdict. Matching is the easy, mechanical part. Deciding what to do about a match is a policy question that needs a human, a context, and an appeals process — as Gorwa et al. (2020) argue at length.

What these numbers cannot tell you

A short list, stated plainly, because a measurement tool that oversells itself does more damage than no tool at all.

  • They cannot see word order. Shuffle a sentence into nonsense and every readability score stays identical. Try it — the tool suggests exactly this experiment. The formulas measure surface form, not meaning, and this is the cleanest demonstration of it.
  • They cannot see whether you know the words. Cat and cusp are both one syllable. A cardiologist reads a cardiology paper faster than a novel. Domain knowledge dominates, and no formula here can access it.
  • They cannot see cohesion. Two paragraphs with identical scores can differ entirely in whether each sentence connects to the last. This is most of what makes writing followable, and it is invisible to every formula on this page.
  • Optimising them can make writing worse. This is the important one. Because the formulas reward short words and short sentences, you can improve a score by replacing a precise technical term with a vague common one, or by chopping a sentence whose clauses explained their own relationship. DuBay (2004) and Bailin & Grafstein (2016) both document this. The score is a symptom, not a target. Treat a bad score as a prompt to reread, never as a specification to satisfy.
  • Style panels are pattern matches, not grammar. Real passive detection needs a part-of-speech tagger, and even then "the door was locked" is ambiguous. We flag candidates, and we pin our known false positives in the test suite so they are documented rather than hidden.
  • The passive voice is not an error. Williams (1981) defends it whenever it puts familiar information first — "the vaccine was developed in 1955" is better than leading with a laboratory nobody has heard of. A tool that reports passives as mistakes teaches a superstition.
  • English only. Every formula, the syllable counter, and the sentence splitter are English-specific. The word counter is Unicode-aware and will happily tokenize other scripts, which means glasstext will produce confident, meaningless numbers for text it cannot actually analyse. Do not trust it outside English.

Using this well

A workflow that respects the limits above:

  1. Look at the longest sentence first. It is on the Style tab. This single number does more for prose than any score.
  2. Read the spread, not the average. Uniform sentence lengths read as flat regardless of the grade level. Readability formulas use only the mean and are blind to this entirely.
  3. Use the grade range as a smoke alarm. If your six formulas cluster around 9 and your audience is a general one, fine. If they cluster around 19, something is genuinely heavy — go and look at what.
  4. Check nominalisations when a sentence feels sluggish. Williams' test: if the real action of the sentence is trapped inside a noun like implementation, turning it back into a verb usually shortens the sentence and sharpens it.
  5. Never rewrite to hit a number. See above. Repeatedly.
